# Sun's $90 identity: a proved differential equation, with the value still open

## Target and eligibility

Define

$$
B_n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk\binom{n+2k}{2k}\binom{2k}k(-324)^{n-k},
\qquad A_n=\binom{2n}nB_n.
$$

Prove the exact identity

$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(357n+103)A_n}{2160^n}=\frac{90}{\pi}.}
$$

## 1. Exact generating function

Set $t=-324$. Writing $n=j+k$ gives

$$
B_n=\sum_{j+k=n}\frac{(j+3k)!}{j!(k!)^3}t^j.
$$

For each fixed $k$,

$$
\sum_{j\ge0}\frac{(j+3k)!}{j!(k!)^3}(tz)^j
=\frac{(3k)!}{(k!)^3}(1-tz)^{-3k-1}.
$$

Since $(3k)!/(k!)^3=27^k(1/3)_k(2/3)_k/(k!)^2$, we obtain the formal identity

$$
\boxed{B(z)=\sum B_nz^n=\frac1{1+324z}
{}_2F_1\left(\frac13,\frac23;1;\frac{27z}{(1+324z)^3}\right).}
$$

Each coefficient in this reindexing receives finitely many contributions, so the formal argument does not assume convergence. Define

$$
F(z)=\sum A_nz^n, \qquad\theta=z\frac{d}{dz}.
$$

Then $F$ is the coefficientwise (Hadamard) product of $B(z)$ and $(1-4z)^{-1/2}$. The target is

$$
(357\theta+103)F(1/2160)=90/\pi.
$$

## 2. A proved fourth-order operator

As a formal power series, $F$ satisfies $RF=0$, where

$$
R=\sum_{j=0}^4z^jP_j(\theta)
$$

and the polynomials are

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_0(T)&=T^3(T-1),\\
P_1(T)&=6T(1170T^3-135T^2+743T+106),\\
P_2(T)&=3888(2T+1)(2223T^3+2268T^2+1949T+648),\\
P_3(T)&=272097792(2T+1)(2T+3)(17T^2+24T+12),\\
P_4(T)&=881596846080(T+1)(2T+1)(2T+3)(2T+5).
\end{aligned}
$$

The coefficients begin $A_0=1,A_1=-636,A_2=583740$. The coefficient of $\theta^4$ in $R$ factors as

$$
(1+864z)(1+3240z)(1+2916z+2519424z^2).
$$

This is **not claimed to be the minimal-order equation**. A finite coefficient fit alone would not prove even that it annihilates $F$. We used the following algebraic argument to certify it.

### Step A: derive a second-order equation for $B$

Write

$$
h=1-tz,\quad g=1+2tz,\quad Q=h^3-27z,
$$

$$
N=g^2(h^3-54z)-6tz(1+tz)Q.
$$

The chain rule applied to the hypergeometric equation

$$
r(1-r)y''+(1-2r)y'-\frac29y=0,
\quad r=27z/h^3,\quad y=hB,
$$

gives

$$
c_2B''+c_1B'+c_0B=0,
$$

where

$$
c_2=zh^2gQ, \quad c_1=hN-2tzhgQ, \quad c_0=-6g^3-tN.
$$

The verifier reconstructs the chain-rule coefficients directly and checks equality as rational functions. It does not rely on matching a finite list of $B_n$.

### Step B: transform to an exact equation $LF=0$

Expand

$$
(c_2/z)\theta(\theta-1)+c_1\theta+zc_0
=\sum_{j=0}^6z^jp_j(\theta).
$$

Its coefficient recurrence transforms under $A_n=\binom{2n}nB_n$ using

$$
\frac{\binom{2n}n}{\binom{2(n-j)}{n-j}}
=\prod_{r=0}^{j-1}\frac{2(2n-2r-1)}{n-r}.
$$

Multiply by $n(n-1)\cdots(n-5)$ and cancel the resulting common polynomial factor (n-5). This is valid for $n\ge6$; **all six exceptional indices $0\le n\le5$ are checked exactly**. The transformed recurrence yields a seventh-order polynomial differential operator $L$ with $LF=0$.

### Step C: exact operator factorization and initial data

Ordinary differential operators compose noncommutatively. Using the Leibniz rule, exact rational-function division gives

$$
L=\mathcal Q\circ R,
$$

with zero remainder. The quotient  $\mathcal Q=\sum_{j=0}^3q_j(z)D^j$, $D=d/dz$, is saved in the [certificate JSON](../results/sun90/operator_certificate.json). Each $q_j(z)/z^j$ is regular at zero, with limits $-24,18,-6,1$ for $j=0,1,2,3$. Therefore its indicial polynomial is

$$
I(\nu)=-24+18\nu-6\nu(\nu-1)+\nu(\nu-1)(\nu-2)
=(\nu-2)(\nu-3)(\nu-4).
$$

Right divisibility alone is insufficient. Set $H=RF$. We know $\mathcal QH=0$, and direct exact evaluation gives

$$
[z^0]H=[z^1]H=\cdots=[z^4]H=0.
$$

If $H\ne0$, let $\nu\ge5$ be its first nonzero exponent. The first coefficient of $\mathcal QH$ equals $I(\nu)[z^\nu]H\ne0$, a contradiction. Thus **$RF=0$**.

An independent AI reviewer checked the argument and reran the exact verifier: [review](sun90_operator_review.md). The executable and mathematical reasoning establish an operator identity; no external human referee or formal proof assistant is claimed.

## 3. A convergent integral and an equivalent remaining target

An independent approach obtained, with $a=1296z$ and $f(q)={}_2F_1(1/3,2/3;1;q)$,

$$
F(z)=\frac1{\pi\sqrt{1+a}}\int_0^1
f\left(\frac{108z(1-u)(1+au)^2}{(1+a)^3}\right)
\frac{du}{\sqrt{u(1-u)}}.
$$

The [full derivation](sun90_integral.md) uses Euler's hypergeometric transformation and a beta integral. Absolute convergence at $z_0=1/2160$, and in a neighborhood, follows by bounding the double series with

$$
\frac1{1-3/5}  f\left(\frac{25}{32}\right)<\infty.
$$

This justifies reordering and differentiation near the target. At $z_0$, let

$$
q(u)=\frac{(1-u)(3u+5)^2}{2048},\qquad Y(u)=f(q(u)).
$$

The argument lies in ([0,1/81]); its maximum is at $u=1/9$. Carrying the (z)-derivative through the integral shows that the prize identity is equivalent to the exact, still unproved evaluation

$$
\boxed{\int_0^1\left[\frac{577}{16}Y(u)
-\frac{1785}{8}(1-u)Y'(u)\right]
\frac{du}{\sqrt{u(1-u)}}=36\sqrt{10}.}
$$

The algebraic factorization

$$
1-q(u)=\frac{(u+7)(9u^2-42u+289)}{2048}
$$

may be useful for an elliptic transformation. Its $7$/$15$ structure is a clue, **not** a proved complex-multiplication identification. The cubic is not monotone on the full interval; a substitution must handle both branches. Naive integration by parts introduces a divergent endpoint term at zero.

## 4. What failed and what to do next

The first fourth-order operator was recovered from 45 exact coefficients, then checked on 55 unused coefficients. That suggested a candidate but did not prove it. The argument above is what elevates the candidate to a theorem. Modular searches found no order-two or order-three fit within the tested low-degree ansatz; this does not establish minimality or rule out algebraic pullbacks and products.

A simple $P_n(U)P_n(V)$ Legendre-polynomial ansatz did not match further coefficients. Standard Brafman/Bailey product formulas and the MacMahon–Franel connection were investigated, but no exact transformation at the required special point was completed. See [literature and failed approaches](sun90_literature.md).

The strongest next step is to identify a valid product of two elliptic periods for this function (or integral), prove that transformation with differential equations and initial data, and then establish its special values and derivative relation. An alternative is a complete WZ certificate for a suitable parameterized identity. Decimal agreement, an apparent singularity guess, or a newly fitted operator does not supply the missing evaluation.
